Aruṇam is the first chapter (praśna) of the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka, a Vedic section intended to be chanted in a forest like, secluded setting; in modern life this is interpreted as a quiet, isolated corner of one’s home used for focused recitation (adhyayanam).
The name “Aruṇam” is explained in two ways: (1) a pañcati states that the seers (ṛṣis) associated with these mantras are called Aruṇa, hence the collection is Aruṇam; (2) it deals with the Aruṇa ketuka yajña, thus acquiring the same name.
Aruṇam comprises 132 pañcatis, each roughly 50 words; these units are pedagogical, used in traditional teaching (chandai and then personal practice called tiruvai), and may not align with complete sentences but help memorization.
The 132 pañcatis are grouped into 32 anuvākas; after each anuvāka practitioners typically recite a short praise to Śrī Sūrya Nārāyaṇa along with “Om Namo Nārāyaṇāya” and offer namaskāras, with some traditions adding Sūrya Navagraha Sūktam or Āditya Hṛdayam at the end.
The thematic structure of the 32 anuvākas is outlined:
1–6: Sun as the embodiment of time (kāla svarūpa).
7: Sun in Aṣṭamūrti form and description of Mahā Meru.
8: Death and ways to avert it.
9: Apamṛtyu, i.e., avoiding untimely death and attaining full lifespan.
10: Pairs of opposites (day–night, hot–cold) viewed as Śiva–Śakti divine couple.
11: Gāyatrī and Śrī Vidyā upāsanā.
12–22: Various mantras, including Rudra gaṇas, Bhūmadevī mantras, ending with Mantra puṣpam.
23–26: Aruṇa ketuka yajña and its brick arrangement (chayana).
27–31: Means to mokṣa (mokṣa sādhana) and the Vaiśravaṇa yajña.
32: Saṃvatsara vrata, completing the section.
Each praśna in the Āraṇyaka begins with a distinct śānti mantra; Taittirīya Upaniṣad (praśnas 7–9) starts with “Saha nāv avatu,” whereas Aruṇam starts with the “Bhadraṃ Karṇebhiḥ” śānti mantra, which is also repeated again within the first pañcati for the specific Aruṇa ketuka ritual.
The śānti mantra is explained as a prayer for the removal of three classes of obstacles (tāpatraya) during study and ritual: ādhyātmika (from oneself and close relations), ādhibhautika (from environment and beings), and ādhidaivika (from natural or divine forces like storms or earthquakes).
