The passage presents a clear and structured explanation of the third Pañcati of the first Anuvāka, guided by the traditional understanding that all Vedic study begins and ends with reverence to the Guru. The opening Guru mantras establish the Guru as the source of knowledge and realization, identifying the Guru with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Maheśvara, and ultimately with Parabrahman itself. This framing emphasizes that correct understanding of the mantras is possible only through disciplined teaching and transmission.

The main focus of the explanation is the worship of the waters, referred to as the Jalābhimāni Devatās. The four mantras analyzed ask the waters to act as protective and sustaining forces for both the world (bhuvana) and the Devatās. Through word-by-word interpretation, important terms such as vajra (Indra’s thunderbolt), Ajita (unconquerable), and Aditya (the Devatās born of Aditi) are explained. The waters are requested to place the vajra in the sky, symbolizing divine protection from above, and to help the worshipper remain unconquered by harmful forces such as Rakṣasas. The role of Aditi as the mother of the Devatās is highlighted to show how cosmic order is preserved through her lineage, while destructive forces arise from a different source.

The explanation then moves to the concluding set of mantras, which ask the waters to be auspicious, peaceful, and beneficial. These mantras seek well-being not only for people, but also for plants, herbs, and the surrounding environment. Sarasvatī is invoked both as the goddess of knowledge and as a symbol of sacred waters, showing the close connection between speech, wisdom, and purity. The phrase mā te vyoma sandṛśi is interpreted as a request for continuous protection, with no separation between the Devatās and those performing the ritual.

The passage concludes by clarifying the deeper meaning of this worship. Although the prayers are addressed to the waters, the ultimate object of worship is Sūrya. Since waters originate from Agni, and Agni is linked to the solar principle, worship of the waters becomes an indirect worship of Sūrya. With this understanding, the first Anuvāka is completed, and the text prepares to move into the second Anuvāka with the Pūrṇam mantra, affirming completeness and balance in Vedic thought.
