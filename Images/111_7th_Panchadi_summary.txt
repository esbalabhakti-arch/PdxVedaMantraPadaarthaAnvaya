The seventh Panchadi presents a symbolic and metaphysical exposition of Samvatsara—the cosmic Year—as an expression of Kāla Puruṣa, the Personification of Time. The opening phrase “Ubhayataḥ Sapta Indriyāṇi” describes the year as possessing “seven sense organs on both sides.” This imagery builds on earlier depictions of the cosmic being whose northern (Uttarāyaṇa) and southern (Dakṣiṇāyana) paths function as its two faces. The “seven indriyās” are interpreted not as abstract faculties but as seven apertures (dvāras), analogous to the human face: two eyes, two nostrils, two ears, and one mouth. The text thus establishes a correspondence between the macrocosm (the cosmic year) and the microcosm (the human organism). Just as human experience is mediated through seven gateways of perception, the cosmic year manifests through observable divisions and transitions.

The subsequent line, “Jalpitaṁ tveva dihyate,” offers an epistemological reflection. It suggests that what is spoken about (jalpitaṁ) is only what is seen (dihyate). Human discourse remains confined to perceptible phenomena. Applied to the context of the year, this implies that people describe its visible features—its solstitial divisions, seasons, and cyclical rhythms—but rarely contemplate its underlying metaphysical cause. The teaching implicitly critiques a purely empirical outlook and encourages inquiry into the transcendent principle behind temporal cycles. Thus, the Panchadi moves from cosmological imagery to philosophical introspection.

The text then introduces the duality of śukla and kṛṣṇa, interpreted as the bright and dark fortnights (śukla pakṣa and kṛṣṇa pakṣa), or alternatively as day and night. These represent the right and left sides (dakṣiṇa and vāma) of the cosmic year. By describing these divisions as the “sides” of Samvatsara, the Panchadi reinforces the anthropomorphic metaphor while emphasizing cyclical complementarity. The year is not static but structured through alternating phases of light and darkness, growth and withdrawal. Yet the phrase “tasya eṣā bhavati” signals that further elaboration follows, indicating that these dualities are preparatory to a deeper insight about the nature of time itself.

The verse “Śukraṁ te anyat, yajyataṁ te anyat” further differentiates the two halves: one bright, the other characterized as worthy of worship. The dark fortnight’s association with ritual and ancestral observances suggests that decline and obscurity possess sacred significance. Time’s darker phase is not negation but an integral dimension of cosmic order. This insight destabilizes simplistic valuations of light over darkness and affirms the theological unity of opposites within temporal manifestation.

A significant philosophical turn appears in “Viśvarūpe ahanī dyauḥ iva asi.” Here, the two forms—day and night—are described as equal in measure, and the cosmic principle is said to stand between them like the mid-space between heaven and earth. This metaphor conveys transcendence amidst immanence. Just as the atmosphere exists between earth and heaven without belonging exclusively to either, the supreme principle underlying Samvatsara pervades the dualities of time without being bound by them. The Panchadi thus intimates a non-dual metaphysics: temporal distinctions are real at the empirical level but grounded in a transcendent unity.

The concluding lines invoke the sustaining power behind temporal cycles: the protector and nourisher who, through māyā, manifests and upholds the world. Addressed as Pūṣan, this principle provides sustenance and ensures continuity. The prayer “Bhadra te Pūṣan iha rātir astu” seeks auspiciousness from this cosmic sustainer, recognizing that the ordered functioning of time is itself a beneficent gift. Overall, the seventh Panchadi weaves cosmology, epistemology, and theology into a unified reflection: while human perception apprehends time through observable dualities, true understanding lies in discerning the transcendent principle that underlies and harmonizes them.
