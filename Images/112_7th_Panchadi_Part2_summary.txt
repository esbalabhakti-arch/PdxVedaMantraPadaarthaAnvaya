The latter portion of the seventh Panchadi marks a decisive philosophical shift from cosmological symbolism to metaphysical negation and spiritual instruction. Having previously described the cosmic year (Samvatsara) as the manifest form of Kāla Puruṣa—structured through bright and dark fortnights and sustained by Pūṣan—the text now deepens its inquiry into the ontological status of these manifestations.

The striking sequence beginning with “Na atra bhuvanam, na Pūṣā, na paśavaḥ, na Ādityaḥ” introduces a radical negation. Literally translated, these statements deny the existence of the world, the nourishing deity Pūṣan, living beings, and even the sun. At first glance, such assertions appear contradictory to the earlier devotional praise of the cosmic sustainer. However, the negation operates at a higher philosophical level. It reflects a Vedāntic standpoint in which empirical reality (vyavahāra) is ultimately sublated by absolute truth (paramārtha). The world and its governing principles are not denied in a practical sense; rather, they are declared non-ultimate—projections sustained by māyā. Thus, the Panchadi transitions from reverence toward cosmic order to insight into its metaphysical ground.

The statement “Samvatsara eva pratyakṣeṇa priyatamaṁ vidyāt” observes that the year alone is directly perceived and regarded as most beloved. Human beings relate intimately to the cycles of time because they are immediately accessible and experientially meaningful. Seasonal rhythms, ritual calendars, and the alternation of bright and dark phases shape lived experience. The following affirmation—“Etat vai Samvatsarasya priyatamaṁ rūpam”—declares these perceptible forms to be the most cherished manifestations of the year. Yet this attachment to the visible form also signals limitation: what is loved is what is seen, not the transcendent cause behind it.

The text then addresses the condition of the individual self (jīva), described as one who must undertake “mahān artha”—great and beneficial actions. These actions are not merely worldly pursuits but prescribed sacred duties (karmas) aligned with Dharma. The imperative “Idam puṇyaṁ kuruṣva” (“Perform this meritorious act”) carries an exhortative tone. Rituals such as yajñas—including the Aruṇa Ketuka Chayana referenced in the discourse—serve a dual purpose. They yield tangible benefits within the empirical world and, more importantly, purify the mind (citta-śuddhi). Such purification prepares the aspirant to discern the Paramātman beyond temporal appearances.

The concluding instruction, often summarized as “Tam āharaṇam dadyāt,” reinforces the necessity of performing these ordained actions. Spiritual realization is not achieved through negation alone but through disciplined engagement with Dharma. The Panchadi thus articulates a progression: from perception of time’s manifest structure, to recognition of its metaphysical unreality, to disciplined action that refines the seeker’s consciousness.

Traditional exegesis interprets this Panchadi in two complementary dimensions: the Saṁhitā aspect and the Brāhmaṇa aspect. The Saṁhitā portion poetically portrays the cosmic year as Kāla Puruṣa, structured by dualities and sustained by divine nourishment. The Brāhmaṇa dimension internalizes this symbolism, revealing that these forms are ultimately māyā and that the true seeker must transcend attachment to perceptible phenomena. Through prescribed actions and contemplative insight, one advances from empirical devotion to realization of the underlying Paramātman.

With this synthesis, the seventh Panchadi concludes the second Anuvāka of the Aruṇam. It bridges ritual cosmology and non-dual metaphysics, guiding the aspirant from observable temporal cycles toward the timeless fullness that underlies them.
