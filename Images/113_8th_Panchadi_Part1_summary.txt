Following the completion of seven Panchadis within the first two Anuvagams, the discourse transitions into the third Anuvagam, beginning with the eighth Panchadi. This section focuses on the cosmological nature of solar radiation and its relationship to the earthly environment. The text introduces the term Sakam Janam, which commentators identify as the rays or light originating from the Sun. Central to this discussion is the concept of Saptatam, which carries dual interpretations: it either refers to a specific seventh ray that is "born first" and binds the others, or it signifies the seven wavelengths that collectively appear as a single, unified white light. These rays are described as Devajāḥ, or "God-born," emerging directly from the deity Surya.

The Rshis explain that while the light is composed of seven elements, six specific rays (Sat) are particularly significant in their interaction with the physical world. These six rays are assigned specific "abodes" or "places" (Dāmasaḥ) arranged by Surya Devata. In a practical sense, these abodes correspond to the six Ritus (seasons) defined in the Shastras, such as Grīṣma (summer), Varṣā (monsoon), and Śiśira (winter). Each ray governs a specific seasonal cycle, which is traditionally comprised of two months.

These solar forces do not exist in a vacuum but are said to "rejoice" or "shine forth" (Rejante) for the specific benefit of the world. Each ray manifests through a unique Rūpam—a distinctive form or nature (svabhāvam)—that dictates the atmospheric conditions of its corresponding season, such as the characteristic heat of the summer months. This framework illustrates a divine order where solar energy is systematically divided and directed to maintain the various seasonal states of the planet.

The discourse concludes by addressing the epistemological challenge of believing these scriptural assertions. Recognizing that observers may doubt how seven rays can be responsible for such specific earthly cycles, the text prepares to offer an analogy. This intended comparison serves to bridge the gap between Vedic observation and the skepticism of those who do not yet trust the authority of the Arunam or the Shastras regarding the fundamental nature of reality.
