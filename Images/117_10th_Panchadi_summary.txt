The 10th pañcati recording begins with the standard guru stotra and then moves immediately into chanting and explaining a short but dense section that links social conduct in Vasant ṛtu (spring) with the broader structure of the seasons and their distinct qualities. The central ideas are: how to treat guests during auspicious functions, how to recognize Vasant ṛtu from the kinds of words and activities that occur, and how each ṛtu has its own specific “viśeṣaṇa” (distinctive features), with the text then introducing Grīṣma ṛtu (summer) and its association with Rudras and intense heat.

The first line, “amūghaś ca parirakṣata,” is explained as an instruction regarding “those who have come from far places.” The form “amūghaḥ” is taken to refer to such guests, and “parirakṣata” is glossed as “protect them well; take good care of them.” The teacher connects this to the final line of the previous, ninth pañcati – “amūn ādayate anyān” – which also spoke of “these people” and “others” to be taken care of. Together, they convey that, especially during Vasant ṛtu, when functions like marriages and upanayanams are frequent, the yajamānas (hosts) instruct their relatives, helpers, or sevakas to ensure that all guests are properly fed and that those who have travelled long distances receive particular care and protection.

The next verse, “etā vācaḥ prayujyate yatra etad upadṛśyate,” is unpacked as a general statement about such instructions. “Etā vācaḥ” (“these words”) refers to phrases like “feed them well,” “look after them carefully,” and similar expressions of hospitality and concern. “Prayujyante” is explained as “are used, are applied,” and “yatra etad upadṛśyate” as “where these words or activities are seen/heard.” The key interpretive move is that when such language and acts of careful hosting are commonly heard and observed, one should infer that Vasant ṛtu has arrived. In other words, the social atmosphere of busy auspicious functions, guest care, and repeated injunctions to protect and feed visitors becomes a practical “signal” of the season.

This idea is made explicit in “etad eva vijānīyāt pramāṇaṃ kāla‑paryaye.” The phrase is glossed as, “this alone one should know as the pramāṇa (proof or indication) of the passage of time (kāla‑paryaya) through the seasons.” The text is teaching that the observable patterns of human activity and speech themselves serve as an authoritative indicator of which ṛtu is current. The speaker emphasizes that every ṛtu has its own guṇas and viśeṣaṇas—its particular qualities, both natural and social—which distinguish it from the others.

The following line, “viśeṣaṇaṃ tu vakṣyāmaḥ ṛtūnāṃ tan nibodhata,” promises that these distinctions of the ṛtus will be systematically explained in the coming verses and exhorts the listener to understand them. Up to this point, Vasant ṛtu has been described in terms of its festive, hospitable character and earlier (in prior recordings) as arriving with red garments, clean waters, and the Aṣṭa‑Vasus. The 10th pañcati now turns to the next season, Grīṣma ṛtu.

The final lines introduce Grīṣma: “śukla‑vāsā rudra‑gaṇaḥ gṛīṣmeṇa āvartate saha nijahan pṛthvī sarvā.” Here, the “Rudra‑gaṇa,” a group of eleven Rudras, are said to be “śukla‑vāsā” (clad in white garments) and to arrive together with Grīṣma ṛtu. As summer begins, it is presented as if the world is accompanied by these white‑robed Rudras, and “nijahan pṛthvī sarvā” is interpreted as the entire earth being scorched by their heat: the sun’s rays become fiercely hot, and the land burns under their influence. The teacher briefly cites differing Purāṇic lists of the eleven Rudras (from Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Mahābhārata) to illustrate the scriptural richness behind the phrase “Rudra‑gaṇa.”

The recording closes by summarizing the contrast between the two seasons described so far. Vasant ṛtu comes adorned in red, with the Vasus and pure waters, marked by hospitality and joyous functions. Grīṣma ṛtu follows, accompanied by the eleven Rudras in white and characterized by intense, burning heat. Each ṛtu thus has a distinct personality, both in its natural phenomena and in the human activities that surround it, and the text uses both to teach how time’s passage through the year can be “read” and understood.
