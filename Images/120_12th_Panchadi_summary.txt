The twelfth Panchadi opens the fourth Anuvakam with a vivid and poetic description of the Sharad ritu, the autumn season, contrasting it with the difficulties brought on by the preceding Varsha ritu, the monsoon season. Through carefully layered Sanskrit verses and their interpretations, the text paints a picture of renewal, clarity, and abundance that defines this transitional time of year.
The opening lines draw upon the powerful imagery of the eyes. Just as eyes that have been afflicted with pain or disease experience profound relief and clarity once healed, so too does the world emerge clearer and more vibrant with the arrival of Sharad ritu. This is not merely a poetic flourish — it carries practical significance. The Varsha ritu was associated with ailments such as red eye and yellow fever, conditions that caused suffering among people and animals alike. With the coming of autumn, these diseases recede, and the world is described as entering a state of freedom from sorrow and illness.
Building on this imagery, the text notes that with clear, healthy eyes now free from the tears and obstructions of pain, people begin to adorn and decorate them — a reference to the traditional practice of applying dark collyrium or anjana around the eyes. This act of beautification signals not just physical wellbeing, but a broader mood of celebration and joy that the season brings with it.
Central to this Panchadi is the introduction of the Ribhus, the divine craftsmen of the Vedic tradition. These three celestial beings are credited with the transformation that Sharad ritu brings. They are described as adorned in new, unworn garments that shimmer with a golden radiance, symbolizing their divine nature and the brightness they bring to the season. The Ribhus are understood to actively remove the blemishes and impurities left behind by the monsoon, restoring harmony and beauty to the world. The three Ribhus named here are Ribhu, their leader, associated with ingenuity; Vibhavan, associated with brilliance; and Vaja, associated with strength and abundance.
The Panchadi then turns its attention to the social and communal experience of the season. Words of nourishment and care become characteristic of Sharad ritu — encouragements to eat clean and wholesome food, to purify oneself, and assurances of abundant life. The verse states that when one hears such utterances — "eat well," "cleanse yourself," "I shall provide you with life and sustenance" — it is a sign that the autumn season has truly arrived. These are not just practical instructions but carry a spiritual undertone, reflecting the idea that the season itself becomes a source of vitality and renewal for all living beings.
Taken together, the twelfth Panchadi presents Sharad ritu as a season of healing, beauty, divine craftsmanship, and nourishment. It is a time when the hardships of the monsoon are left behind, when people emerge refreshed and adorned, when the divine Ribhus work their craft upon the world, and when life-giving abundance flows freely — making it one of the most celebrated and auspicious seasons in the Vedic understanding of nature's cycle.
