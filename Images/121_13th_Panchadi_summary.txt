The 13th Panchādi of the Āruṇa Praśnam reveals the sensory and elemental nature of Hemanta Ṛtu—a season defined by the biting chill and restless motion of the wind gods, the Marut Devatās. Originating from the Vāyu Loka, these cold winds sweep across the earth like arrows released from a bow, striking every living being with piercing discomfort. The hymn vividly compares this cold assault to an army advancing in formation—“Sannaddhāḥ saha dadr̥śe ha”—where people stand shoulder to shoulder seeking warmth, enveloped in rough, dull-colored or sheepskin garments that act as protective armor (kavacha) against the wind’s sharp sting.

The imagery unfolds with dynamic metaphors of battle. The chill of Hemanta is portrayed as a relentless, weapon-like force, and the huddled beings resemble soldiers defending themselves from an unseen adversary. Even the natural appearance of creatures adapts—their disheveled coverings, worn and dusty like old leather, evoke both the rustic gloom and resilience of early winter. The Maruts, sons of Kashyapa and Diti, embody this powerful and tumultuous energy; though born from Diti’s wish for vengeance against Indra, they were transformed by divine will into companions of Indra, governing the winds that dominate this season.

In the concluding imagery, the hymns describe eyes reddened by the cold—Lohinī iva vidyāt—as if filled with the anger of one ready for battle. Though there is no real wrath, the piercing chill causes a redness and dryness resembling the eyes of a warrior preparing for combat. Thus, anger becomes a metaphor for the intensity of cold, showing how nature itself mimics human emotion under harsh conditions.

Overall, this Panchādi presents Hemanta Ṛtu as a vivid interplay between the fierce breath of the Maruts and the instinctive struggle of beings to endure, expressing both the physical severity and the spiritual vigor that winter brings.
