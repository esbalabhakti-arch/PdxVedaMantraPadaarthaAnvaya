The 16th Panchadi of Aruna Prashnam explains the glory of the Visvedevas' bow through a series of mythological narratives, culminating in a ritual teaching about protection from Rudra's wrath.
The Rainbow as Indra's Bow (Indradhanush)
The Visvedevas possessed a magnificent bow, and Indra, taking the form of a termite (vamri), secretly cut its bowstring. With the string severed, the bow lost its tension and became stringless — and this stringless bow is what we observe in the sky as the rainbow (indradhanush). The Sanskrit is precise: jya means bowstring, and ajyam means without a string, which is exactly what the bow became after Indra's act. The multicolored arc visible in clouds is understood as this very bow, stripped of its string by Indra's cunning.
The Bow's Connection to Other Deities
The text further establishes the bow's cosmic significance by linking it to multiple divine figures. Sayu, a son of Brihaspati (the preceptor of the gods), also wielded this same bow, underscoring its importance across the heavenly realm. More significantly, Rudra himself used the Visvedevas' bow — hence the rainbow is also sometimes called Rudradhanush. The bow thus carries the identity of multiple great deities simultaneously.
Rudra's Head is Severed
The narrative takes a dramatic turn when describing what happened after the war between Rudra and the Asuras. Having defeated his enemies, Rudra was resting with his chin placed upon one end of the bow (the artnhi, or tip of the bow). At this very moment, Indra — in his termite form — cut the bowstring. The sudden release of tension caused the bow's tip to spring back violently, striking and severing Rudra's head from his body. This is the central mythological event the panchadi builds toward.
The Head Becomes the Pravargya Rite
Rather than remaining a tragedy, Rudra's severed head was transformed into something sacred. It became the basis for the Pravargya, a preparatory rite performed within the Jyotishtoma Yajna. In this important Vedic sacrifice, the Pravargya rite symbolically represents the head of the sacrificial offering. The severed head of Rudra, in essence, became consecrated and embedded within Vedic ritual tradition.
Protection Through Ritual Performance
The passage concludes with a powerful teaching: whoever performs the Pravargya rite within the Jyotishtoma Yajna is considered to be symbolically reattaching Rudra's head to his body. This act of ritual restoration earns the performer complete protection — Rudra will not harm or punish that person. The final line, "ya evam veda" (one who knows thus), affirms that this knowledge itself carries transformative power.
In essence, this panchadi weaves cosmology, mythology, and ritual together — explaining the rainbow's origin, Rudra's vulnerability, and the redemptive power of the Pravargya ceremony within a single, unified teaching.
