The 17th Panchati of the sixth Anuvakam of the Arunam, provides a detailed, personified depiction of Shishira Ritu (the winter season). The text describes Shishira as a Purusha (personified deity) whose physical gestures are entirely dictated by the bitter cold. The opening lines, अ॒त्यू॒र्ध्वा॒क्षो-ऽति॑रश्चात् । शिशि॑रः प्र॒दृश्य॑ते । (meaning looking excessively upward and sideways) , describe how individuals in this season raise their eyebrows and move only their eyes to look around, deliberately keeping their heads and bodies still to preserve warmth. Furthermore, the passage नैव रूप-न्न॑ वासा॒ग्ं॒सि । न चक्षुः॑ प्रति॒दृश्य॑ते । highlights that during this freezing period, people completely disregard their physical appearance, the aesthetics of their form, or the fashion of their garments. Bundled up closely to survive the dropping temperatures, their eyes and features remain largely obscured, as the singular, overriding focus of every individual shifts entirely to staying warm.  

The commentary then transitions to the social behaviors and the divine characteristics (Devalakshanam) associated with this freezing period. In the line अ॒न्योन्य॒-न्तु न॑ हिग्ग्​स्रा॒तः । स॒त स्त॑-द्देव॒लक्ष॑णम् । the text beautifully emphasizes that despite any challenging circumstances that may arise, people choose not to fight or harm one another. They actively avoid conflict to save their physical energy for survival. Moving to the visual imagery of the Abhimana Devata (the presiding deity of the season), the verse लोहितो-ऽक्ष्णि शा॑रशी॒र्​ष्णिः । सू॒र्यस्यो॑दय॒न-म्प्र॑ति । presents a striking metaphorical comparison. At the exact time of sunrise, the deity of Shishira Ritu is visualized with a green head and starkly contrasting red eyes. This specific aesthetic is compared by the Bhashyakara (commentator) to the vibrant, sharp-headed appearance of a green parrot with distinct red eyes, painting a vivid cosmic picture of the winter dawn.  

The final segment of the Panchati illustrates the instinctual physical postures adopted by human beings to combat the extreme winter chill. Through the phrases त्व-ङ्करोषि॑ न्यञ्ज॒लिकाम् । त्व॒-ङ्करो॑षि नि॒जानु॑काम् ॥ the text explores the unique, inverted gestures of the body during this time. While an Anjali mudra normally denotes an outward salutation or prayer facing another person , the intense cold forces an "opposite" action : people fold their arms tightly against their own chests to trap their body heat, effectively performing a self-directed Anjali. Similarly, Nijanukam refers to kneeling down and bending the joints , but rather than a formal prostration, it describes drawing one's knees tightly up to the chest. The commentary evokes the vivid image of people gathering closely around a fire, extending their hands to catch the warmth, and then bringing those warmed hands back to touch their own bodies, crouching low to endure the rigorous winter.
